I parked under the third streetlight because the first two buzzed.

That was the rule I made that night. I made a new rule most nights. Rules were easier than plans. A plan needed a tomorrow, and I wasn't sure I had one of those. A rule only needed the next ten minutes.

The lot belonged to a strip mall that had given up. A nail salon with paper over the windows. A tax place. A sandwich shop that closed at nine and left its neon OPEN sign burning anyway, like it forgot. I backed the Civic into the last space so the trunk faced the wall. Nobody could walk up behind me. Old habit. The kind the Army puts in you and never takes back out.

I turned the engine off. The heat came in right away, even after dark. It sat on my chest like a second blanket.

I counted the streetlights. Seven along the front of the lot. Two on the side. Nine.

I counted again. Nine.

Good. The numbers were behaving tonight.

My phone said eleven percent. I turned the brightness all the way down and opened my contacts and looked at Emma's name for a while. I didn't call. I never called. I just liked knowing the number still worked, the way you like knowing a door is there even if you'll never open it.

I put the phone face down on the passenger seat.

The watch on my wrist ticked. My father's watch. I could hear it when the lot was quiet, a small steady sound under the hum of the lights. I'd started setting my breathing to it. Four ticks in. Four ticks out. The VA therapist had taught me the breathing. The watch was my idea.

Around midnight a car pulled into the lot.

Headlights swept across the windshield and I was awake all the way, fast, like somebody had thrown cold water on the inside of my skull. The car rolled past the sandwich shop. Slow. It stopped at the far end, under the first streetlight. The buzzing one.

Engine running. Lights off.

I didn't move. Moving is how people see you.

I tried to read the shape. Sedan. Dark color. Dark windows, or maybe just dark, because everything was dark. I couldn't see a driver. I couldn't see anything except the faint orange from the lamp sliding over the roof.

Nobody got out.

Four ticks in. Four ticks out.

I told myself the reasonable things. Somebody's waiting for a friend. Somebody's on the phone and doesn't want to do it at home. Somebody's lost and checking a map. People sit in parked cars all the time. I was sitting in a parked car. That didn't make me a threat. It made me tired.

The doctors had a word for what my brain did. They had a lot of words. Hypervigilance was one. It meant my head was a smoke detector that went off when you made toast. It meant I couldn't trust the alarm.

So I didn't trust it. I watched the car anyway.

Ten minutes. Fifteen.

The sedan didn't leave. The OPEN sign flickered once and came back. A plane crossed the sky, blinking red, low and slow.

I picked up the phone without turning it over. I wanted the weight of it in my hand. Eleven percent was enough for one call. I ran through who it would be. Not the cops. The cops would look at the garbage bags in my back seat and the sleeping bag and the empty bottles and decide which one of us was the problem. Not Emma. Not my father.

That was the list. That was the whole list.

At twelve thirty-one, by my father's watch, the sedan's brake lights came on. Red across the asphalt. Then off. Then the car rolled forward, slow, all the way down the row toward me.

It passed close. Close enough that I could have touched the paint if my window had been open. I didn't look at it. I looked at my own hands on the steering wheel. They were shaking. They always shook now.

The car turned out onto the road and the sound of it went away, and the lot was just the lot again. Hum. Buzz. Tick.

I sat there until my heart came down.

Then I did the thing I'd started doing, the thing I didn't tell anyone about. I opened the glove compartment and took out the pen I kept there and wrote on the back of a gas receipt.

12:04. Dark sedan. Far end. Lights off. Nobody out.

12:31. Left. Drove past me.

I looked at what I'd written. In the dark it didn't look like much. Two lines. A crazy man's grocery list.

But in the morning I'd forget. That was the part nobody understood. In the morning the whole thing would be gone, or it would be changed, or I'd remember it as a dream. The receipt would still say what it said. Paper doesn't have a brain injury.

I folded it and put it in the glove compartment with the others.

There were a lot of others.

I lay back across the seat and tucked the sleeping bag under my head. The watch ticked. The streetlights hummed. I counted them one more time to be sure.

Nine.

Then ten.

I sat up.

Then nine again, and I lay back down, and I told myself it was the brain, it was only ever the brain, and I watched the far end of the lot until the sky went gray.
