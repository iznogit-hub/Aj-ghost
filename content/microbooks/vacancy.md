The sign out front said VACANCY in red letters, except the V had been dead since summer, so at night it said ACANCY, and Lorna had stopped noticing.

She worked the desk from ten to six at a two-story motel near the edge of town, the kind with outdoor stairs and an ice machine that groaned like an animal every forty minutes. People paid by the night. Some paid by the week. Lorna didn't ask anybody why. In her experience, nobody who checked in at midnight wanted to be asked why.

At 11:40 a man came in.

He stood just inside the glass door for a second before he walked up to the counter, the way people do when they're letting their eyes adjust. Or the way people do when they're checking who else is in the room. There was nobody else in the room. There was Lorna, and the coffee pot nobody drank from, and the little TV on the wall playing a cooking show with the sound off.

"How much for one night?"

She told him. He nodded, took out a fold of bills, and counted them on the counter. Then he counted them again. His lips moved the second time.

"Ground floor," he said. "If you have it. Facing the lot."

"So you can see your car?"

He looked at her for a moment like she'd read his mind. Then he almost smiled. "Yeah."

She gave him fourteen. She slid the key across. He picked it up, and then he didn't leave.

"Has anybody asked about me?" he said.

Lorna blinked. "You just got here."

"Right." He rubbed the side of his head, just above his right ear, like something hurt there. "Right. Sorry. Long day."

"Long night," she said, and he did smile at that, a little, before he went out.

At 2:05 a black sedan pulled into the lot.

Lorna saw it on the camera monitor first, a gray shape on a gray screen, and then she looked up through the glass door and saw it for real. It parked across from the ground floor rooms. Right across from fourteen. Lights off. Engine running. She could see the faint exhaust in the cold air.

Nobody got out.

Nobody came to the desk.

People pulled into motel lots at two in the morning for a lot of reasons. Lorna had seen most of them. Somebody waiting for somebody. Somebody too drunk to drive the last mile home and smart enough to know it. Somebody making a phone call they didn't want anyone to hear.

Has anybody asked about me?

She didn't want to think about that. She thought about it anyway.

The notepad was at the far end of the counter, and she didn't want to move, didn't want the motion to show through the glass. So she picked up the pen and wrote the plate on the inside of her own wrist, the way she used to write phone numbers in high school. She had to squint. The sign above the lot only lit half of it. She got the letters. She got most of the numbers.

At 2:40 the sedan backed out, slow, and pulled onto the road and was gone.

Across the lot, the curtain in fourteen moved. Just an inch. A sliver of a face. Then the curtain went still.

He'd been awake the whole time. Of course he had.

At 5:30, before the sky even started to go pale, the man from fourteen came back to the desk with the key. He'd showered. His hair was wet. He looked like he hadn't slept at all.

Lorna took the key. She thought about not saying anything. That was the rule, the one nobody wrote down. You don't get involved.

"There was a car out there last night," she said.

He went very still. "Black?"

"Yeah."

"You saw it?" He said it carefully, like the answer mattered more than the question. "You actually saw it."

"I saw it. I didn't see the driver."

He let out a breath that seemed to come from somewhere very deep. "I thought maybe I dreamed it," he said. "I do that sometimes."

"I wrote down the plate." She turned her wrist over to show him.

They both looked at it.

The ink had smeared. Her own skin, her own sleeve, an hour of rubbing at her wrist without noticing. The letters were still there. The numbers were a blur. A seven, maybe. Or a one. Maybe a four that had lost its top.

She felt stupid. She started to apologize.

"It's okay," he said. He was still looking at her wrist. "The numbers do that to me too."

Then he thanked her, and he went out to his car, and he checked every door handle before he got in, and he drove away. She watched him turn onto the road in the same direction the sedan had gone.

She washed her wrist in the bathroom sink at the end of her shift. Then she stood there with the water running, looking at her clean skin, trying to remember the plate.

She couldn't.

The next night the V was still dead. ACANCY. She noticed it for the first time in months.
