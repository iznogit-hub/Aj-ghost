Marisol drove the last run of the night, the one that left the transit center at eleven fifty and came back around one, mostly empty.

She liked it. People thought she was crazy for liking it. But the last run was quiet, and the city at that hour belonged to the people who had nowhere else to be, and she had spent enough years with nowhere else to be that she didn't mind their company.

There was a man who got on at the stop outside the closed furniture store. Same stop every night for two weeks. Hard to say how old. Clean jacket that didn't fit right, like it came from a donation bin. He always paid in exact change, coins counted out in his palm before the doors even opened. He always sat in the first sideways seat, the one facing the aisle, with his back against the window.

Not facing forward. Not facing back. Facing the door.

Marisol had a brother who sat like that in restaurants. She didn't ask the man about it.

He rode to the end of the line every night and walked off toward the parking lots behind the hospital. She figured he slept somewhere out there. She figured that was his business.

On the Thursday it rained, which it almost never did, she noticed the car.

Black sedan. It was behind the bus when she pulled away from the furniture store. That wasn't strange. Cars got stuck behind buses all the time. They honked, they swerved around, they cursed you out the window.

This one didn't swerve around.

At the next stop she pulled to the curb and opened the doors, and nobody got on, and nobody got off, and in her side mirror the sedan pulled to the curb too. Twenty feet back. Headlights on. Wipers going.

She closed the doors. Pulled out. The sedan pulled out.

Next stop. Same thing.

She looked in the big mirror over the windshield, the one that showed her the whole inside of the bus. Three riders. A woman asleep against the window in a grocery store uniform. A kid with headphones. And the man in the sideways seat, very still.

He wasn't watching the door anymore. He was watching the back window. He'd seen it too.

Their eyes met in the mirror. He looked away first.

At the fifth stop Marisol did something she wasn't supposed to do. She didn't pull to the curb. She stopped in the lane, hazards on, opened the front door, and waited.

Behind her, the sedan stopped too. It had nowhere to go. There was a parked truck on its right and the bus in front of it, and for a second she felt good, felt clever, like she had caught something by the tail.

Then the sedan's driver's window came down.

Not all the way. A few inches. She couldn't see a face. Rain, and the glare of headlights in her mirror, and the dark inside the car. But a hand came out, palm up, and caught a little rain, the way you would if you were checking whether it was still coming down.

Then the hand went back in and the window went up.

It was a nothing gesture. It was the most normal thing in the world. It made the back of her neck go cold.

She closed the door and drove.

At the next light the sedan turned right and was gone, smooth, signal on, like any car on any road on any night. Marisol watched the empty mirror for three more stops.

At the end of the line the woman in the grocery uniform woke up and got off, and the kid with the headphones got off, and the man in the sideways seat stood but didn't move toward the door.

"You saw it," he said.

It wasn't a question. His voice was low and careful, like he was setting something fragile on a table.

"I saw a car," Marisol said.

He nodded. He looked down at his hands. They were shaking a little, and he pressed them flat against his legs to stop it.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Thank you."

"For what?"

He didn't answer right away. He thought about it. She could see him thinking about it.

"For saying it was there," he said.

Then he got off the bus and walked into the rain toward the dark lots behind the hospital, and he didn't look back, and she didn't watch him go, because she'd been raised to think that watching somebody walk away was a kind of rudeness.

The next night he wasn't at the stop outside the furniture store.

Or the night after that.

She told her supervisor about the sedan, finally, and her supervisor asked if the car had hit the bus, and she said no, and he asked if anybody had been hurt, and she said no, and he asked what exactly she wanted him to write down.

She didn't have an answer for that.

She still drives the last run. She still looks at the stop outside the furniture store, every night, out of habit. Sometimes there's someone there. Sometimes they sit facing the door.

She always waits a little longer than she has to before she pulls away.
