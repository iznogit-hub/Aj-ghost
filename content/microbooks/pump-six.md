The overnight shift at the station had three rules, and Teo had written none of them.

Don't leave the register. Don't open the door after two unless it's a cop. Don't get involved.

He was twenty-three and four months into the job, and he had gotten very good at the third one.

At one in the morning the store was all hum. The coolers hummed. The lights hummed. The hot dog roller turned the same four hot dogs it had been turning since ten. Outside, the canopy lit the pumps like a stage, and past the edge of the light the lot went dark fast, as if somebody had cut it with scissors.

There was an old Honda parked out there in the dark. Far corner, by the air pump that didn't work. It had been there three nights in a row.

Teo knew the type. Everybody who worked nights knew the type. Somebody sleeping in their car. Usually they came in once, used the bathroom, bought nothing or bought one coffee and made it last. Usually they were gone by six. His manager said to call it in if they stayed past a week.

This one hadn't come in at all. That was the strange part. Teo had seen him only once, walking back to the Honda with a gallon of water from somewhere else, a tall guy with his shoulders up near his ears like it was cold. It wasn't cold. It was never cold.

At 1:14 a black sedan pulled in under the canopy and stopped at pump six.

Nice car. Clean. Dark windows. The kind of car that doesn't need gas at a place like this, because the kind of person who drives it gets gas somewhere with a car wash and good coffee.

Nobody got out.

Teo waited for pump six to light up on his screen. Pay at the pump, or prepay inside. Neither happened. Pump six just sat there, idle, with the black car beside it like a dog sitting next to a bowl it had no plan to eat from.

He rang up a guy buying cigarettes and a lottery ticket. He said have a good night. He looked up again.

Still there.

From the register he could see the driver's side, and the window was down maybe two inches. Not enough to see a face. Enough for a thin line of light from a phone screen. Then the light went away and something else moved, slow and careful. A hand holding a pen. A small notebook resting on the steering wheel.

Writing.

Teo followed where the windshield was pointed.

The far corner. The Honda.

He told himself it could be anything. A repo guy. An insurance thing. A private investigator, which Teo knew about only from TV, but still. Somebody checking up on somebody. People wrote things down. That wasn't a crime.

But the sedan had parked at pump six, and pump six had the best view of the far corner. He'd never thought about that before. Now he couldn't stop thinking about it.

At 1:40 the Honda's dome light came on. Just for a second. Teo saw the shape of the guy sitting up, one hand pressed flat to the side of his head, the way you'd hold a door shut against the wind. Then the light went off again.

In the sedan, the pen moved.

Teo's phone was in his back pocket. He took it out and held it under the counter where the camera couldn't see, which was stupid, since nobody watched the footage unless something got stolen. He opened the keypad.

What would he say? There's a car at my pump that isn't buying gas. There's a man sleeping in my lot who hasn't stolen anything. There's somebody writing in a notebook.

He could hear how it would sound. He could hear the dispatcher's voice, polite and tired.

He put the phone back.

At 2:10 the sedan's engine turned over, smooth and quiet, and it rolled out from under the canopy. It didn't take the exit to the street. It went the long way, around the back of the lot, past the dumpsters, and slow along the dark edge where the Honda sat. Slow enough that Teo held his breath. Then out the side driveway and gone.

The Honda didn't move.

At six, when his shift ended, Teo did something he had never done in four months. He bought a bottle of water with his own money and walked it out to the far corner of the lot.

The guy was asleep, folded across the front seat. Garbage bags in the back. The windows fogged a little from his breathing. Teo stood there with the water in his hand, feeling dumb, feeling like he should knock and not wanting to.

He set the bottle on the hood instead, where the guy would see it.

On the way back to his own car he passed pump six. There was something on the ground beside it. A small square of paper, torn from a notebook, pinned under the edge of the island where the wind had pushed it.

He picked it up.

It was just numbers. Times. 1:14. 1:40. 2:10. And next to 1:40, two words in small, neat handwriting.

Still awake.

Teo looked at the Honda. Then at the street. Then at his own hands, which he noticed were not quite steady.

He put the paper in his pocket. He did not throw it away. He did not call anyone, either.

Nobody ever looks, his manager liked to say. That's what makes night shift easy.

Teo had always thought it was a joke.
